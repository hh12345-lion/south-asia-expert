Country information is not always uniform. Different sources may describe the same issue in different ways, particularly where circumstances vary by location, community, period or source.

For solicitors preparing an asylum or immigration case involving South Asia, translated and transliterated material can raise questions about meaning, context and source identification.

A South Asian country expert may consider the original-language material, any available translation, the circumstances in which the material was produced and its relevance to the specific issue under consideration. The approach will depend on the nature of the material and the question the expert has been asked to address.

This article considers some of the issues that may arise when translated or transliterated country evidence is relevant to an expert report.

## What Is the Difference Between Translation and Transliteration?

Translation and transliteration are different processes.

### Translation

Translation involves conveying the meaning of words or text from one language into another language.

For example, a Bengali news report may be translated into English so that its contents can be considered by lawyers, experts and the tribunal.

The translation may need to communicate not only individual words but also the meaning of expressions, terminology and sentences in their original context.

### Transliteration

Transliteration generally involves representing words from one writing system using another script.

A person's name, place name or organisation may therefore appear in several different forms when written in English.

For example, a Bengali, Hindi, Nepali or Sinhala name may have more than one English spelling depending on the transliteration system used.

A transliteration is not necessarily a translation. It may simply represent the pronunciation or written form of the original term using a different script.

## Why Language Can Matter in Country Expert Evidence

The meaning of country information can sometimes depend on language and context.

A direct English-language search may not identify all material concerning a particular event, person, organisation or locality. Local-language sources may contain information that is not readily available in English.

This can be relevant where the expert has been asked to consider a specific country condition, locality, community or event.

Language knowledge may also assist an expert in identifying differences between a literal translation and the wider meaning of an expression.

## Types of Material That May Require Translation

Different forms of country evidence may appear in languages other than English.

### News Reports and Local Media

Local media can report events occurring in particular towns, districts or communities.

Where such reporting is relevant to an expert's assessment, the expert may need to understand the original report and the context in which it was published.

This can be particularly relevant where an English-language summary provides only limited information about the original article.

### Government and Official Documents

Government documents, notices and other official material may be published in local languages.

Depending on the country and the document concerned, terminology used in an official document may have a particular administrative or legal meaning.

An expert may therefore consider the original document alongside any English translation available.

### Court or Police Documents

Documents relating to courts, police authorities or other institutions may also contain terminology that does not translate neatly into English.

Where such material forms part of the evidence provided for consideration, the expert may need to distinguish between the contents of the document and any interpretation of what those contents mean in the relevant country context.

### Local Organisations and Community Sources

Reports from local organisations, community groups or other local sources may also be published in languages other than English.

The relevance of such material may depend on its provenance, subject matter, date and relationship to the particular country issue being examined.

## How an Expert May Approach a Translated Source

The treatment of a translated source may depend on what the source is being used to establish.

### Identify the Original Source

Where possible, it can be useful to identify the original document or publication rather than relying only on an English-language version.

This may allow the expert to consider:

- The original title
- The date of publication
- The publisher or issuing body
- The surrounding text
- The original terminology
- Any relevant geographical or institutional references

Identifying the source can also help distinguish between the original material and later summaries or references to it.

### Consider the Translation

A translation may communicate the substance of the original material while leaving some expressions open to interpretation.

An expert who understands the original language may be able to consider whether particular terms have a broader or narrower meaning in context.

However, where the expert is not responsible for providing a formal translation, the report should not necessarily treat the expert's language knowledge as equivalent to a certified translation.

The appropriate approach can depend on the purpose for which the translated material is being relied upon.

### Consider Context

Individual words may have different meanings depending on how they are used.

This can arise with political terminology, administrative language, titles, names of organisations, descriptions of social groups and other country-specific expressions.

An expert may therefore consider the surrounding context rather than relying solely on an isolated translated term.

## Dealing With Different English Spellings

Names and places in South Asian country evidence may appear in more than one English form.

This can happen because of different transliteration systems, historical spelling conventions or differences between local and English-language publications.

### Personal Names

A person's name may be written differently across documents.

For example, the same individual may appear under different arrangements of given names and family names, or with different representations of sounds from the original language.

An expert may consider whether apparently different spellings could refer to the same person before drawing conclusions from the documents.

### Place Names

The same locality may also appear under different English spellings.

This can occur where a local-language name has been transliterated using different conventions.

Where location is important to the case, identifying the locality accurately may help the expert understand the geographical context of the evidence.

### Organisation Names

Political parties, community organisations and other bodies may also have alternative English spellings or abbreviated names.

An expert may need to establish whether different names refer to the same organisation or to separate organisations.

This can be particularly relevant where the country issue concerns political affiliation or community membership.

## When Translation and Transliteration Create Uncertainty

Not every language issue can necessarily be resolved from the material available.

There may be circumstances where:

- The original document is unavailable
- Only an English translation has been provided
- The source uses an unusual transliteration
- A person's name appears differently across documents
- A place has more than one commonly used English spelling
- A particular expression does not have a direct English equivalent

Where uncertainty remains, an expert may identify the limitation rather than treating an uncertain interpretation as established fact.

## What a Country Expert May Add to Translated Country Information

The role of a country expert is not necessarily limited to translating individual words.

Where the expert has appropriate country and language knowledge, the expert may be able to consider the material within its wider country context.

For example, the expert may be able to explain:

- The significance of a particular local term
- Whether two names may refer to the same locality
- The context in which an organisation operates
- How a particular administrative term is used
- Whether a reported event fits with other available country information
- Any limitations affecting interpretation of the source

Any such opinion should remain connected to the specific question on which expert evidence is required.

## Information Solicitors May Provide When Instructing an Expert

When translated or transliterated material is relevant to a case, solicitors may wish to identify it clearly within the instruction materials.

Depending on the case, useful information may include:

- The original document where available
- An existing English translation
- The date and source of the material
- The name of the person, organisation or locality referred to
- Any alternative spellings already appearing in the case documents
- The particular issue for which the material is relevant
- Any apparent inconsistency between documents

Clear instructions can help the expert understand whether the issue concerns the meaning of the source, its country context, identification of a person or place, or another specific question.

Solicitors can review the [How to Instruct](/how-to-instruct) information for further details about briefing a South Asian country expert.

## Distinguishing Translation From Expert Opinion

It can be important to distinguish between translating a document and providing an expert opinion about what the document means in its country context.

A translation concerns the meaning of the original text in another language. Expert evidence may go further by considering the significance of that material in light of relevant country knowledge.

These are separate functions.

For example, an English translation may establish what a document states, while a country expert may be asked to consider the significance of the document within the relevant political, social, administrative or geographical context.

The precise scope of the expert's role will depend on the instruction and the evidence available.

## Keeping the Original Material Traceable

Where translated material is relied upon, maintaining a clear connection between the original source and the translated version can assist with transparency.

Where practicable, the report or supporting evidence may identify:

- The original source
- The language in which it was produced
- The date of the source
- The translated version used
- Any material uncertainty concerning the translation or identification

This can make it easier to understand how the information has been considered.

## Conclusion

Translated and transliterated country evidence can raise issues that extend beyond the literal meaning of individual words. Names, places, organisations, administrative terminology and local expressions may appear differently across sources, while the wider context can affect how particular material is understood.

A South Asian country expert may consider the original source, available translation, terminology, provenance, date and wider country context when these matters are relevant to the expert question.

Where uncertainty cannot be resolved, identifying the limitation may be more appropriate than presenting an interpretation as certain. For solicitors, providing the original material where available and clearly identifying the specific issue requiring expert consideration can help keep the assessment focused.
